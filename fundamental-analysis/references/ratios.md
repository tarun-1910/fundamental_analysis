# Ratios, Formulas, and Sector Adjustments

Rules of thumb are starting points, not pass/fail tests. Always compare with the company's own history and sector peers.

## 1. Growth and profitability

| Metric | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| CAGR | (End / Start)^(1/years) - 1 | Use 3, 5, 10 year windows |
| Gross margin | Gross profit / Revenue | Stability signals pricing power |
| Operating (EBITDA) margin | EBITDA / Revenue | Compare with peers |
| Net margin | Net profit / Revenue | Watch for one-off gains |
| ROE | Net profit / Average shareholders' equity | Consistently above 15% is strong; check it is not inflated by leverage |
| ROCE | EBIT / (Total assets - Current liabilities) | Should exceed cost of capital by a clear margin; best for comparing capital-intensive firms |
| ROA | Net profit / Average total assets | Most useful for banks and asset-heavy firms |
| DuPont | ROE = Net margin x Asset turnover x Equity multiplier | Shows whether ROE comes from margin, efficiency, or leverage |

## 2. Balance sheet and liquidity

| Metric | Formula | Rule of thumb |
|---|---|---|
| Debt-to-equity | Total debt / Shareholders' equity | Below 1 is comfortable for most industries; higher can be normal for utilities and infrastructure |
| Net debt / EBITDA | (Debt - Cash) / EBITDA | Above 3-4x is stretched for most sectors |
| Interest coverage | EBIT / Interest expense | Below 3x is a warning; below 1.5x is risky |
| Current ratio | Current assets / Current liabilities | Roughly 1.2-2 is healthy; depends on sector |
| Quick ratio | (Current assets - Inventory) / Current liabilities | Stricter liquidity test |

## 3. Cash flow and efficiency

| Metric | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Free cash flow (FCF) | Operating cash flow - Capex | Should be positive and growing over time for mature firms |
| Cash conversion | Operating cash flow / Net profit | Persistently below ~0.7-0.8 needs explaining |
| FCF yield | FCF / Market cap | Compare with bond yields |
| Receivable days | Receivables / Revenue x 365 | Rising trend = collection or revenue-quality concern |
| Inventory days | Inventory / COGS x 365 | Rising trend = demand weakness or obsolescence |
| Payable days | Payables / COGS x 365 | Very high may mean stretching suppliers |
| Cash conversion cycle | Receivable days + Inventory days - Payable days | Shorter is generally better |
| Asset turnover | Revenue / Average total assets | Efficiency of asset use |

## 4. Valuation multiples

| Multiple | Formula | Best used for |
|---|---|---|
| P/E | Price / EPS | Profitable, stable earners; use trailing and forward |
| PEG | P/E / Expected EPS growth (%) | Growth stocks; around 1 or below is often considered reasonable, but it is sensitive to the growth estimate |
| P/B | Price / Book value per share | Banks, insurers, asset-heavy firms |
| EV/EBITDA | (Market cap + Debt - Cash) / EBITDA | Comparing firms with different debt levels |
| EV/Sales | EV / Revenue | Early-stage or low-margin businesses |
| Dividend yield | Dividend per share / Price | Income stocks; check payout is covered by FCF |
| Earnings yield | EPS / Price (inverse of P/E) | Compare with risk-free bond yield |

**Simple DCF skeleton**
1. Project free cash flow for 5-10 years under explicit growth and margin assumptions.
2. Choose a discount rate (cost of equity or WACC) that reflects the risk of the business.
3. Terminal value = FCF in final year x (1 + g) / (discount rate - g), with g at or below long-run GDP/inflation growth.
4. Discount all values to present, subtract net debt, divide by shares outstanding.
5. Run bear/base/bull cases and a sensitivity table (discount rate vs terminal growth). Small input changes move DCF results a lot, so present a range.

## 5. Sector-specific adjustments

**Banks and NBFCs** - Standard debt ratios do not apply because debt is raw material. Use: Net interest margin (NIM), CASA ratio, gross and net NPA, provision coverage ratio, capital adequacy ratio (CAR/CET1), credit cost, loan growth, ROA (about 1-1.5%+ is good for banks) and ROE, P/B and P/E. For NBFCs also check asset-liability mismatches and funding concentration.

**Insurance** - Embedded value, value of new business (VNB) margin, combined ratio (general insurers), solvency ratio, persistency ratio. Valuation by P/EV or P/B.

**IT and services** - Revenue growth in constant currency, EBIT margin, attrition, client concentration, deal wins, utilization, free cash flow conversion. Typically asset-light with high ROCE.

**FMCG and consumer** - Volume vs price growth, distribution reach, gross margin stability, advertising spend, working capital (usually negative or low). Premium multiples are common; check they are supported by consistent growth.

**Pharma** - R&D spend and pipeline, regulatory (USFDA) observations, share of regulated markets, patent expiries, domestic vs export mix.

**Auto and cyclicals (metals, cement, chemicals, shipping)** - Earnings swing with the cycle. Use mid-cycle or normalized earnings, EV/EBITDA, replacement-cost or P/B. A low P/E at peak earnings can be a trap; a high P/E at trough earnings can be cheap.

**Real estate** - Pre-sales and collections, inventory overhang, net debt, approvals pipeline, NAV-based valuation. For REITs: distribution yield, occupancy, lease expiry profile, LTV.

**Infrastructure and utilities** - Higher leverage is normal; focus on regulated returns, contracted cash flows, order book, counterparty risk, and debt servicing coverage (DSCR).

**Early-stage or loss-making firms** - Use revenue growth, gross margin trend, cash burn and runway, path to profitability, unit economics (CAC, LTV, retention), and dilution risk. EV/Sales is the usual multiple; be explicit that valuation is highly uncertain.

## 6. Common red flags

- Profit growing while operating cash flow stays flat or falls
- Receivables or inventory growing much faster than revenue
- High or rising promoter pledging; promoter selling at scale
- Frequent equity dilution or repeated fundraising despite reported profits
- Auditor resignation, qualified opinion, or delayed results
- Large related-party transactions or loans to group companies
- Other income forming a big share of profit
- Frequent changes to accounting policies or one-off "exceptional" items every year
- Debt rising faster than earnings, or short-term debt funding long-term assets
- Margins collapsing while peers hold steady
- Unusual contingent liabilities or pending litigation relative to net worth
